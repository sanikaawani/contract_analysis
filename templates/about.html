1. Problem Statement & Legal Context

Small and medium-sized businesses (SMBs) represent the backbone of commercial supply chains but are disproportionately exposed to contractual hazards. Hiring commercial legal counsel for every vendor or client contract costs between INR 15,000 and INR 75,000 per review—an unviable expense for fast-moving startups and small enterprises.

As a result, SMBs frequently execute standard boilerplate agreements containing insidious legal traps:

  • Evergreen Auto-Renewal: Multi-year rollovers with exit penalties or <15-day non-renewal windows.
  • Uncapped Liabilities & Indemnities: Assumption of indirect, special, and consequential losses.
  • Section 27 Indian Contract Act Non-Competes: Post-termination restraints of trade that hinder the SMB's commercial livelihood.
  • Unilateral Termination: 24-hr exit rights with forfeiture of work-in-progress compensation.
  • Section 74 Punitive Liquidated Damages: Arbitrary daily penalties (2-5% per day) exceeding reasonable compensation.

5. Comprehensive Viva Voce Questions & Detailed Answers

Viva Preparation Guide

Category A: NLP Pipeline & Linguistic Techniques

Q1: What is the difference between Stemming and Lemmatization, and why did you implement both Porter and Lancaster stemmers?

Answer: Stemming is a crude heuristic process that chops off suffixes from words (e.g. "indemnified" → "indemnifi" in Porter), which often results in non-dictionary roots. In contrast, Lemmatization uses vocabulary and morphological analysis with WordNet to return base dictionary lemmas (e.g. "indemnified" → "indemnify"). We implemented both Porter (standard rule-based reduction) and Lancaster (more aggressive iterative reduction) to demonstrate comparative over-stemming and under-stemming metrics during contract clause classification.

Q2: Why is standard NLTK stop word removal dangerous for legal contract NLP, and how did you solve it?

Answer: Standard stop word lists remove words like "not", "never", "without", "unless", "except", "shall". In legal agreements, removing "not" completely reverses liability from "Party A shall not be liable" to "Party A shall be liable"! We solved this by creating a domain-adapted legal stop word filter that preserves all conditional negations and modal verbs while filtering conversational noise.

Q3: How does Part-of-Speech (POS) Tagging help detect contract risk?

Answer: We focus heavily on Modal Verbs (MD). Words like "shall" and "must" represent mandatory legal imperatives that enforce strict liabilities, whereas words like "may" or "can" represent permissive liberties. POS tagging allows our risk engine to quantify the density of mandatory covenants versus permissive rights to compute contractual balance.

Q4: What is Chunking and how is it used in Legal Obligation Framing?

Answer: Chunking (shallow parsing) groups consecutive words into syntactic constituents like Noun Phrases (NP) and Verb Phrases (VP). We created a custom RegexpParser grammar: CLAUSE: {<NP><VP><NP>} that extracts structured obligation frames: [the Service Provider] [shall indemnify and hold harmless] [the Client], clearly isolating the Obligor, Action, and Obligee.

Q5: What entities does your Named Entity Recognition (NER) pipeline extract from Indian contracts?

Answer: Our NER engine uses spaCy (en_core_web_sm) augmented with Indian legal regex boosters to extract 6 critical entity classes: 1. Organizations (Pvt Ltd, LLP, Ltd), 2. Signatories (Person names, Managing Directors), 3. Key Dates (Effective, Renewal, Expiry), 4. Money Sums (INR ₹ amounts, Lakhs, Crores), 5. Durations (months, years, days), and 6. Governing Acts (Indian Contract Act 1872, Arbitration Act 1996, IT Act 2000).

Q6: How does Word Sense Disambiguation (WSD) using the Lesk algorithm resolve legal polysemy?

Answer: Polysemous words have multiple meanings. For example, "consideration" in everyday English means thoughtful kindness, but under Section 2(d) of the Indian Contract Act, it strictly means price or value given in exchange for a promise. The Adapted Lesk algorithm computes word overlap between dictionary gloss definitions and the sentence context to disambiguate and assign the correct legal sense.

Category B: Feature Engineering & Word Embeddings

Q7: Compare Bag of Words (BoW), TF-IDF, and Word2Vec in your project.

Answer: - BoW builds sparse count vectors of raw word frequencies without term importance weighting. - TF-IDF scales term frequency by Inverse Document Frequency ($\log(N/df)$), penalizing generic words and highlighting discriminative legal keywords. - Word2Vec produces dense continuous 100-dimensional vectors capturing semantic similarity (e.g. cosine distance between "rescind" and "terminate"), capturing meaning beyond exact keyword matches.

Q8: Why did you use N-Gram Analysis (Unigrams, Bigrams, Trigrams)?

Answer: Single words (unigrams) often miss contractual context. For instance, "renewal" is neutral, but the bigram "automatic renewal" or trigram "indemnify and hold" represents specific, actionable risk markers. N-gram feature matrices enable ML models to learn multi-word legal collocations.

Q9: How was Word2Vec trained, and what parameters were chosen?

Answer: We used Gensim's Word2Vec with Skip-Gram architecture (sg=1), vector_size=100, window=5, and epochs=35 trained across our preprocessed corpus of Indian legal contract clauses. Skip-Gram was chosen over CBOW because it provides superior semantic representations for rare legal terminology.

Category C: Machine Learning Classifiers & Evaluation

Q10: Why did you evaluate Logistic Regression, Random Forest, and Naive Bayes?

Answer: - Logistic Regression is a convex linear model that performs exceptionally well on high-dimensional sparse TF-IDF text matrices. - Random Forest is a non-linear bagging ensemble of decision trees that handles non-linear feature interactions without overfitting. - Multinomial Naive Bayes provides a fast probabilistic baseline utilizing Bayes' Theorem with Laplacian smoothing ($\alpha=0.1$).

Q11: Why is Precision vs Recall trade-off critical in legal risk detection?

Answer: In legal risk assessment, a False Negative (failing to flag a high-risk uncapped liability clause) is catastrophic for an SMB. Therefore, our model optimizes for high Recall on High-Risk classes, ensuring that potential legal hazards are never silently missed.

Q12: How is the composite Contract Risk Score (0-100) calculated?

Answer: We use a weighted composite formula: $\text{Raw Score} = (N_{\text{high}} \times 28) + (N_{\text{medium}} \times 12) + (N_{\text{low}} \times 3)$, normalized against clause count and scaled with penalty boosters if severe triggers (e.g. uncapped liability + unilateral termination) are simultaneously active. Scores $\ge 71$ are classified as High Risk, 36–70 as Moderate Risk, and $\le 35$ as Low Risk.

Category D: Indian Legal Domain & Practical SMB Application

Q13: What is the significance of Section 27 of the Indian Contract Act, 1872 in non-compete clauses?

Answer: Section 27 states that "every agreement by which anyone is restrained from exercising a lawful profession, trade or business of any kind, is to that extent void." Under Indian law, post-termination non-compete clauses are generally unenforceable (unlike in some US jurisdictions). Our system flags post-contract non-competes as high-risk and recommends replacing them with enforceable non-solicitation covenants.

Q14: What is Section 74 of the Indian Contract Act regarding Liquidated Damages?

Answer: Section 74 allows courts to award reasonable compensation not exceeding the penalty amount named in the contract, but punitive penalties that do not reflect genuine pre-estimates of loss are impermissible. Our system flags arbitrary penalties (e.g., 2% per day of delay without cap) and advises capping liquidated damages at 5–10% of milestone value.

Q15: How can an SMB user utilize the generated PDF report?

Answer: The ReportLab PDF report acts as an Executive Risk Triage Document containing the risk score gauge, extracted entity summary, clause-by-clause AI explanations, and a concrete negotiation counter-proposal checklist that the SMB founder or procurement manager can send directly to opposing counsel.
